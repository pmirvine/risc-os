The Planner was the page of Lotus Organizer people remember: a whole year on one spread, a row for each month and a column for each day, with coloured bars across it for holidays, training courses and projects. You chose a colour from the key, dragged across the days, and the bar was painted. This chapter builds it, and puts its bars in the Diary too. It's mostly drawing - a grid, bars, patterns - with one new kind of drag and one new drawing technique, clipping. The result is !Org15.

Planning the planner

What it remembers
Bars. A bar is a kind (one of six, each with its own colour) and the first and last days it covers.

What can happen
A bar is painted by choosing a kind in the key and dragging across days, or added with the New button; it is deleted with the wastebasket or the Delete key; the year is turned with the page corners. Bars show in the Diary on the days they cover, and double-clicking a day on the chart turns the Diary to it.

So a bar is as small as an entry can be:

{ id: 4, key: 3, from: '2026-10-05', to: '2026-10-23' }

A chart of the year

January to June go on the left-hand page and July to December on the right, a row for each month and 31 columns for the days, with the month's short name down the left. The size of a row and a column comes from the size of the page, so the same code draws both halves; grid works it out:

TOP leaves room for the heading and the day numbers, LABEL for the month names and KEYH for the key at the bottom. What's left is shared between six rows and 31 columns. first is the first month on the page: 1 on the left, 7 on the right.

draw draws the two halves with the same function:

Each day is a white cell - or a pale shaded one on Saturdays and Sundays, from D.weekday, which counts from 0 for Monday so the weekend is 5 and 6. Today has a box round it. Months shorter than 31 days stop early (D.daysInMonth), leaving a gap at the end of their row. The day keys are built from the year, the month and the day with padStart - '2026-09-05', not '2026-9-5', or they wouldn't sort as dates.

The key at the bottom has three kinds on each page, each a small sample of its bar and its name. The one you'll paint with (this.pen) has a box round it. As with entries, the page records where each sample is, in this.keys, for clicks.

Drawing the bars

Each month's row is divided into six lanes, one for each kind, so bars of different kinds that overlap can both be seen:

A bar can run on from one month into the next (a holiday from 28 July to 7 August), so for each month's row the bar is cut down to the part inside that month: from whichever is later of its first day and the 1st, to whichever is earlier of its last day and the month's last day - comparing day keys again. Bars that don't touch the month at all are skipped with continue. Each bar drawn is recorded with this.box, so it can be chosen, dragged to the wastebasket or linked, like any other entry. (A bar across two months is recorded twice, once for each part; both have the same ref.)

Colour and pattern

The Style Guide's rule from chapter 13 matters most here: six colours side by side are exactly what people with colour-blindness can't tell apart, and a grey printout loses them altogether. So every kind has a pattern as well as a colour - Holiday plain, Training diagonal stripes, Meeting dots, Project A upright stripes, Project B stripes the other way, Away a line along the middle. One function fills a rectangle in a kind's colour and pattern, and the key, the chart and the Diary all use it:

The pattern's lines are drawn right across the rectangle and beyond, and then cut off at its edges. That's clipping, which chapter 2's ListView used to keep each column's text inside its column. Here it is again, with the two calls that go round it:

  • g.save() remembers everything about how the canvas is drawing - colours, line width, and the clipping area.

  • g.beginPath(); g.rect(x, y, w, h); g.clip(); makes the rectangle the clipping area: nothing drawn from now on appears outside it.

  • g.restore() puts everything back as it was at save, so the rest of the page isn't clipped.

Without clipping, the stripes would have to be worked out to stop exactly at the edges, which is much harder. The patterns are drawn in black at 45% strength, 'rgba(0,0,0,0.45)' - a colour you can see through, as in chapter 11's answer 2 - so they darken whatever colour is underneath.

Painting a bar

A click on a sample in the key chooses the kind to paint with:

Dragging across days is a different kind of drag from chapter 12's. There, you dragged an entry, and Tools.drag handled it: if the drag starts on an entry's box, it moves it or bins it. Here the drag starts on an empty day, so Tools.drag finds no entry and hands the drag to the section's own drag. (So a drag that starts on a bar drags the bar - to the wastebasket, say - and one that starts on an empty day paints.) The Planner's asks the Wimp for a 'point' drag - the pointer on its own, with nothing carried - and waits for where it ends:

const drop = await wimp.drag({ event: ev.pointerEvent,
  type: 'point' });
If it ends on the organiser's window, dayAt gives the day there. The drag might have gone left instead of right, so the two days are put in order with array destructuring (chapter 3), taking the two days in the right order from one of two arrays:

const [a, z] = from <= to ? [from, to] : [to, from];
and a bar of the chosen kind is added. As always, the page doesn't draw it: the collection changes, and the window redraws.

Which day?

dayAt turns a point back into a day, trying each half in turn:

It's the grid sums the other way round: dividing by the row height and column width gives the row and column, and the row and the half's first month give the month. A point beyond the end of a short month (the 31st of June) is no day at all.

Without the mouse

Dragging isn't for everyone, so New (the button, or Insert) asks for a bar in a form: the kind from a pop-up menu, and the first and last days:

The form is made once and kept (??= makes it the first time only). The two dates are sorted with sort() - day keys again - so it doesn't matter which way round they're typed.

Turning the year

The Planner shows one year. Its page corners turn to the year before or after, Today (the toolbox, or Home) goes back to this year. Turning isn't remembered for Back, but leaving the section is, and state gives the year to remember, so Back from the Diary returns to the year you were looking at:

describe - used by the "Delete ...?" question and the links menu - says what a bar is: Project A, 05/10/2026 to 23/10/2026.

Trying it

1 Run !Org15, open the sample Diary and click the Planner tab.

2 Click Meeting in the key, then drag across a few days in June: a dotted green bar appears. Choose Away and drag across the same days again, starting clear of the green bar (a drag that starts on a bar drags the bar): the two bars sit in different lanes.

3 Drag the new Away bar into the wastebasket.

4 Double-click a day inside the October Project A bar. The Diary turns to that week, with Project A on every day the bar covers - in its last week, Monday to Friday only, since the bar ends on Friday 23 October.

5 Click Back: the Planner comes back, at the year you left it. Click the right-hand page corner to see next year's (empty) planner, and the left-hand one to come back.

Summary

  • A planner bar is a kind and two day keys; onDay is two string comparisons.

  • Work out a chart's sizes in one place (grid) and use them both to draw it and to find what's under the pointer.

  • Give colours patterns as well, so they can be told apart without colour.

  • save, clip and restore confine drawing to a shape; rgba(r, g, b, a) is a colour you can see through.

  • A drag that doesn't carry an entry goes to the section's own drag; wimp.drag({ type: 'point' }) waits for where the pointer is let go.

  • Always give the mouse's jobs a keyboard way too: here, New and a form.

Exercises

1 Show how many days each bar covers in the Diary's week view, after its name (Project A (15 days)). D.daysBetween counts the days between two keys.

2 Let the user rename the six kinds. Keep the names in the document (so each organiser can have its own) instead of PLANNER_KEY, and change one by double-clicking its sample in the key.

3 Let a bar be moved: dragging a bar to another day on the chart should move it there, keeping its length.

4 Lotus Organizer's planner folded open. Add a view button, Half, that shows only the six months around today, three on each page, with wider columns.

Answers

1 In DiaryPage.itemsOn, give the bar its length in its text: 
text: `${PLANNER_KEY[b.key]} (${D.daysBetween(b.from, b.to) + 1}` +
  ' days)',
(+ 1 because a bar from Monday to Monday covers the first day too).

2 Keep doc.plannerNames (saved with the rest of the file, defaulting to a copy of PLANNER_KEY), use it wherever PLANNER_KEY[k] is used, and make PlannerPage.double async (it now waits for a form), adding before it looks for a day: 
const k = this.keys.find((r) => inside(r, ev.x, ev.y));
if (k) {
  const v = await this.nameForm().ask({
    name: this.doc.plannerNames[k.k] });
  if (v) this.doc.renameKind(k.k, v.name);
  return true;
}
where nameForm makes (once, with ??=) a one-field Form like New's, and renameKind changes the name and marks the document changed.

3 A bar dragged onto the pages already goes to the section's dropped (chapter 12), which the Planner hasn't got. Give it one: 
dropped(ref, x, y) {
  const day = this.dayAt(x, y), b = this.doc.planner.get(ref.id);
  if (!day || !b) return;
  const len = D.daysBetween(b.from, b.to);
  this.doc.planner.update(b.id, { from: day,
    to: D.addDays(day, len) });
}
The bar's first day goes where it's dropped, wherever on the bar the drag began; a better version would remember which day was under the pointer when the drag started.

4 Give the section views: ['Year', 'Half'] in its constructor, and make grid and draw use the months to show instead of 1 and 7: for Half, the month three before this one and the one after it, three rows each, so rowH is worked out for three rows. dayAt uses the same two first months, so clicks still find the right days.
